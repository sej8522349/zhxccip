How We Chose the Best Prenatal Vitamins

To narrow down the best prenatal vitamins from the hundreds available, we spoke with two registered dietitians: Langhough and Stacey Woodson, RDN, who is also an author.

In addition, the author of this article drew on her experience as a dietitian; a former Women, Infants, and Children program nutritionist; and a mom of three.

We used the following criteria to select the best, highest-quality prenatal vitamins across several format, formulation, and budget categories:

Third-Party Testing Independent lab testing helps ensure that the supplement label accurately represents what’s in the bottle. These labs also test for the presence of contaminants, allergens, and heavy metals.

Nutrient Bioavailability Most nutrients are available in several supplemental forms. All forms are absorbed differently by the body, and some are easier to absorb, called more bioavailable, than others. Most of the products we chose contain highly bioavailable nutrients, although some of the more budget-friendly options contain forms that are not as easy to absorb. These supplements can still be effective, though.

Ingredient Quality We largely chose prenatal vitamins free of unnecessary additives and fillers. For optimal product quality, preservatives, additives, or fillers must be used in some cases. All products on our list are free of artificial sweeteners, colors, and flavors.

Customer Reviews We considered reviews from customers, looking for common themes — both positive and negative — to illustrate the typical experience with each product.

What to Look for When Buying Prenatal Vitamins

It’s important to choose a prenatal vitamin that provides the specific nutrients you need, is easy for you to take, and is within your budget. Here are a few things to consider when shopping for a prenatal vitamin:

Price You’ll likely be taking a prenatal vitamin for most of your pregnancy, even longer if you start taking it prior to pregnancy or continue taking it postpartum. For this reason, you’ll need to choose one that fits comfortably in your budget for this entire period.

Form Prenatal vitamins are available in many forms, including capsules, softgels, chewables, gummies, liquids, and powders. If you have trouble swallowing pills or find it difficult to manage certain forms due to nausea, you’ll want to choose a prenatal form that’s more tolerable.

Nutrients Included Prenatal vitamins aren’t based on a standardized formula, so they can contain different nutrients and quantities. If your healthcare provider has instructed you to increase your intake of a certain nutrient, like iron or DHA, be sure to choose a product that contains it.

Third-Party Testing It’s important to look for prenatal vitamins that are third-party tested. “The supplement industry is not regulated,” Langhough explains. This testing helps ensure that the packaging is an honest representation of what’s in the product and that the supplement is free of potentially harmful contaminants and heavy metals.

What Are the Benefits of Prenatal Vitamins?

Prenatal vitamins are made with pregnant people and babies in mind. They contain more of certain nutrients and less of others “to meet the growing needs of the expectant mother and baby,” says Woodson. They play an extremely important role in a healthy pregnancy.

“The need for certain nutrients increases significantly during pregnancy,” continues Woodson, “and prenatal vitamins are a valuable resource to help meet those growing demands and prevent birth defects.” She adds that they can be particularly helpful for pregnant women who are unable to eat a healthy, balanced diet during their pregnancy due to common pregnancy complications like nausea, vomiting, heartburn, and reduced appetite.

What Are the Most Important Nutrients in Prenatal Vitamins?

Because everyone’s diet is different, prenatal vitamins are well rounded, with a broad selection of nutrients to cover most potential nutrient gaps. Across the board, however, some nutrients are particularly important for a healthy pregnancy:

DHA DHA, a type of omega-3 fatty acid found in fatty fish and ocean algae, is “essential for fetal brain and eye development,” explains Woodson. She says that pregnant people need at least 200 to 300 milligrams a day.

What Are the Side Effects of Prenatal Vitamins?

“Side effects aren’t super common,” says Langhough. “However, prenatal vitamins may cause some gastrointestinal issues like constipation, bloating, nausea, and gas.” Additionally, you may experience nausea or digestive issues throughout pregnancy that exacerbate these issues. When dealing with pregnancy nausea, switching to a prenatal liquid or gummy may help.

Why Is Third-Party Testing Important?

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) provides regulatory oversight of the dietary supplement industry. However, it’s fairly hands-off unless problems arise. The FDA doesn’t approve supplements before they’re available to purchase, so some unscrupulous manufacturers may use this to their advantage to sell poor quality, contaminated products or products that don’t contain what the label states.

Third-party testing — carried out at the expense of the supplement manufacturer — provides evidence that a supplement’s contents match the label and that the supplement is safe to consume. Additionally, manufacturers that are willing to invest in third-party testing are signaling that they are reputable and trustworthy. The best supplement manufacturers share third-party testing reports for each batch directly on their websites for users to review.

FAQ

You can take prenatal vitamins when you’re not pregnant. Langough and Woodson both recommend taking a prenatal vitamin before becoming pregnant to support the intensive growth of your baby during early pregnancy. Most prenatal vitamin formulations help support postpartum and breastfeeding mothers, too.

You should begin taking a prenatal vitamin as soon as you find out you are pregnant, but starting before conception can also be helpful. “Babies rapidly develop in the first two to six weeks of pregnancy,” says Langhough, “so starting a prenatal vitamin 90 days prior to conception can help ensure that mama is replenishing nutrient stores for when the body needs them most.”

Prenatal vitamins can help reduce the risk of certain birth defects or complications. If you don’t take a prenatal vitamin, you may be more likely to experience these issues. Taking a prenatal vitamin is a relatively easy and inexpensive way to improve the chances of a healthy outcome for you and your baby.

You can take prenatal vitamins in the morning or at night, or split your daily dose into multiple smaller doses to take throughout the day. Many women find taking prenatal vitamins with a meal helps reduce digestive discomfort.
