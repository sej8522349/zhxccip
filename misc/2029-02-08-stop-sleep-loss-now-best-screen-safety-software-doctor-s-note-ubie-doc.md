Why Blue Light Affects Sleep

Melatonin suppressionBlue wavelengths (around 460–480 nm) inhibit melatonin production. Melatonin is the hormone that signals to your brain it's time to wind down. When it's low, falling asleep becomes harder.

Circadian rhythm disruptionYour body follows a roughly 24-hour cycle. Late-evening screen use can shift this cycle later, making you feel alert when you should be drowsy.

Eye strain and fatigueExtended exposure to screens—especially in dim rooms—can cause headaches, dry eyes and tension, all of which add to discomfort at bedtime.

By reducing blue light exposure in the evening, you give your body a clearer cue that it's time to rest.

Additional Screen‐Safety Tips

Reducing blue light at the software level is a great start, but consider these habits to further protect your sleep:

Follow the 20-20-20 ruleEvery 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for at least 20 seconds. This relieves eye strain.

Set a digital curfewAim to stop using screens at least 60 minutes before bedtime. Use this wind-down time for reading (on paper), gentle stretching or meditation.

Lower ambient lightDim overhead lights in the evening and use warm-toned lamps to signal to your brain that night is approaching.

Adjust device brightnessMatch screen brightness to room lighting. Excessive contrast forces your eyes to work harder.

Use physical night-mode glassesIf you must use screens late at night, amber-tinted glasses can further block blue wavelengths.

Create a sleep-friendly environmentKeep your bedroom cool, dark and quiet. Consider blackout curtains or a white-noise machine.

Software tools help reduce blue light, but true sleep health involves multiple factors:

Consistent scheduleGo to bed and wake up at the same time every day, even on weekends.
