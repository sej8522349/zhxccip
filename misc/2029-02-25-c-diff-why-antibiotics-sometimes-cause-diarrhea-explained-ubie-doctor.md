# C. diff: Why Antibiotics Sometimes Cause Diarrhea, Explained | Ubie Doctor's Note

C. diff: Why Antibiotics Sometimes Cause Diarrhea, Explained

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Antibiotics can disrupt your gut microbiome by killing beneficial bacteria and altering the intestinal environment, often leading to antibiotic-associated diarrhea. In more serious cases, this imbalance allows Clostridioides difficile (C. diff) to overgrow and release toxins, causing severe inflammation, watery stools, and abdominal pain.

Several factors influence your risk, including the type of antibiotic used, age, hospitalization, and underlying health conditions. Recognizing symptoms early—such as persistent diarrhea, cramping, fever, or dehydration—is critical for timely diagnosis and treatment, which may include stopping the offending antibiotic, starting targeted therapy, or, in severe cases, additional medical care.

Explanation

Antibiotics are powerful tools against bacterial infections, but they don't always come without side effects. One of the most common is antibiotic-associated diarrhea (AAD), which in some cases stems from an overgrowth of Clostridioides difficile (C. diff). This guide explains in clear, straightforward terms why antibiotics can lead to diarrhea, how C. diff fits into the picture, and what you can do to prevent or manage it.

How Antibiotics Disrupt the Gut

Our intestines are home to trillions of bacteria—often called the gut microbiome—that help digest food, produce vitamins, and keep harmful microbes in check. Antibiotics, while targeting disease-causing bacteria, can also disturb this delicate balance:

Broad-spectrum impactMany antibiotics kill a wide variety of bacteria, both "good" and "bad."

Reduction of diversityFewer beneficial species means less competition against opportunistic pathogens.

Altered gut environmentChanges in pH and nutrient availability can favor harmful bacteria.

When the gut microbiome is disrupted, antibiotic-associated diarrhea can occur. In most cases, it's mild and resolves after stopping the antibiotic, but sometimes a more serious infection like C. diff develops.

What Is C. difficile?

Clostridioides difficile is a bacterium found in soil, water, and even the healthy gut in small numbers. Under normal circumstances, C. diff remains harmless. However, when antibiotics decimate competing bacteria, C. diff can:

Turn into hardy spores that survive harsh conditions

Multiply rapidly in the colon

Not all antibiotics carry the same risk. Those most commonly linked to C. diff and AAD include:

Clindamycin

Who's at Higher Risk?

While anyone taking antibiotics can develop AAD or C. diff, certain factors raise the risk:

Age over 65

Recent hospitalization or nursing home stay

Previous episodes of C. diff

Weakened immune system (e.g., chemotherapy, HIV)

Prolonged or multiple antibiotic courses

Use of proton pump inhibitors (acid-reducing medications)

Recognizing Symptoms

C. diff infection (CDI) often appears during or shortly after an antibiotic course. Key signs to watch for include:

Frequent, watery diarrhea (at least three loose stools in 24 hours)

Abdominal cramps or pain

Low-grade fever

Nausea

Dehydration (dry mouth, dizziness, reduced urine output)

Severe cases might involve bloody stools, rapid heart rate, or significant belly swelling. If you notice these, seek medical attention right away.

Diagnosing C. diff

Your doctor may recommend:

Stool tests to detect C. diff toxins or DNA

Blood tests to check for signs of dehydration or infection

Imaging (rarely) if complications like toxic megacolon are suspected

Early diagnosis helps prevent serious complications and guides the right treatment.

1. Stopping or Changing Antibiotics

Whenever possible, your doctor will stop the offending antibiotic.

If you still need treatment for another infection, they may switch to a drug with lower C. diff risk.

2. Targeted C. diff Antibiotics

Vancomycin (oral) or fidaxomicin are first-line treatments.

Metronidazole is less preferred but still used in some settings.

3. Probiotics and Diet

Probiotics like Saccharomyces boulardii may help restore balance.

A bland, easy-to-digest diet (e.g., bananas, rice, applesauce, toast) can reduce irritation.

4. Advanced Therapies

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can be life-changing for recurrent C. diff.

Under medical supervision, stool from a healthy donor is transferred to rebuild gut flora.

Preventing Antibiotic-Associated Diarrhea

You can take steps to minimize the risk of AAD and C. diff:

Only take antibiotics when prescribed and complete the course as directed.

Discuss narrow-spectrum options with your doctor.

Practice good hand hygiene—especially after using the bathroom.

Maintain a balanced diet rich in fiber to support gut health.

Consider probiotic supplements during and after antibiotic therapy.

Beyond C. diff: Other Infections to Watch For

Antibiotics can also disrupt the natural balance of microorganisms in other parts of the body, leading to fungal overgrowth. Women may develop vaginal yeast infections with symptoms like itching, unusual discharge, or discomfort during or after antibiotic treatment. When to Seek Medical Help

While mild AAD often resolves on its own, call your doctor or seek immediate care if you experience:

Severe or bloody diarrhea

High fever (over 102°F/39°C)

Signs of dehydration (rapid heartbeat, lightheadedness)

Severe abdominal pain or swelling

Diarrhea lasting more than 2 days
