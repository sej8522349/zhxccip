When it comes to growing their forearms, plenty of guys start and end with wrist curls and extensions—but as Athlean-X creator Jeff Cavaliere, C.S.C.S. explains, there's a more effective way to start seeing gains in this undertrained muscle.

You can help to grow mass in your forearms by training a muscle called the brachioradialis, which Cavaliere describes as "the triceps of the forearm," even though it's not technically even a part of the forearm, as it doesn't extend any action to the wrist like other forearm muscles. Instead, the brachioradialis aids flexion, supination, and pronation of the elbow.

Once you know where it is, it's obvious when the brachioradialis comes into play when training your arms: The muscle is engaged in any exercise that requires movement of the elbow. According to Cavaliere, in order to get the most out of working this muscle, you should be doing exercises that involve both flexion of the elbow and pronation of the forearm.

"If we want to train the muscle through all of its functions, we want to get pronation and elbow flexion similar to when we do the biceps," he says. "We don't want to just flex at the elbow, we also want to flex with the shoulder. That's where we get the most muscle activation, and to get the greatest gains, you're going to want to both overload with the greatest amount of weight, and activate the muscle as much as you possibly can."

There are a number of specific moves that Cavaliere recommends for training this muscle, such as the reverse barbell curl. You can use a straight bar, which will help you achieve a fully pronated position and requires less weight, but at the cost of muscle activation. Alternatively, you can use an EZ bar, which will put your wrists in a more neutral position and allow you to load more weight while reducing the risk of wrist injury while training the brachioradialis. "My recommendation is to mix them up to get the benefits of both," says Cavaliere. You can also switch barbells for dumbbells, using an offset grip for overloaded pronation.

"The more flexion you get at the elbow, the more activation you get in the muscle," he explains, meaning that exercises which exploit the upper range of motion are the best for working the brachioradialis, such as those which use the lat pulldown machine. A reverse grip lat pulldown, which involves pulling the forearms all the way back behind the head, also employs a pronated position, meaning you can get the maximum benefits without having to load extraneous weights.

Battle ropes are also a great way to train your brachioradialis, as they require flexion of the elbow as standard. Rather than gripping them from beneath, Cavaliere advises gripping heavy ropes from the side, in a neutral position. If they're lighter, he recommends using a pronated grip.
