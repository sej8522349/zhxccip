Low Sperm Count: What Doctors Check and What Can Help

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Low sperm count (under 15 million sperm per milliliter) is diagnosed through a detailed medical history, physical exam, semen analysis, hormone testing, scrotal ultrasound, and genetic studies. These tests help identify common causes, including hormonal imbalances, varicoceles, infections, and obstructions.

Treatment options vary based on the cause and may include lifestyle changes, supplements, medications, surgical procedures, or assisted reproductive technologies. Because symptoms and causes differ from person to person, understanding your specific situation is the critical first step.

Explanation

A low sperm count—also known as oligospermia—is when the number of sperm cells in a man's ejaculate falls below 15 million sperm per milliliter. While this can affect fertility, it doesn't necessarily mean you can't father a child. Modern medicine offers a range of tests, treatments and lifestyle strategies to help improve sperm production and function. This guide explains what doctors look for, what can help and when to seek professional advice.

What Does "Low Sperm Count" Mean?

Normal sperm concentration: 15 million to more than 200 million sperm per milliliter

Mild oligospermia: 10–15 million sperm/mL

Moderate oligospermia: 5–10 million sperm/mL

Severe oligospermia: fewer than 5 million sperm/mL

A low count may be temporary or persistent. It's often one piece of the fertility puzzle alongside sperm motility (movement) and morphology (shape).

Anabolic steroids and certain prescription drugs

What Doctors Check

When you see a specialist—usually a urologist or reproductive endocrinologist—they'll perform a series of evaluations to pinpoint causes and guide treatment.

1. Detailed Medical and Sexual History

Duration of attempts to conceive

Past illnesses (mumps after puberty, STIs)

Medications and supplements

Lifestyle habits (smoking, alcohol, drug use, occupation)

Sexual function and frequency of ejaculation

2. Physical Examination

Testicular size, consistency and position

Examination for varicocele (often a "bag of worms" feel)

Check for hernias, penile abnormalities and signs of hormonal issues

3. Semen Analysis

Volume: Normal is 1.5–5 mL per ejaculation

Concentration: Sperm count per milliliter

Motility: Percentage of moving sperm

Morphology: Percentage of normally shaped sperm

pH level and white blood cell count (to detect infection)

At least two semen samples, collected several weeks apart, are recommended to confirm results.

4. Hormone Testing

Blood tests to measure:

Testosterone

Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)

Luteinizing hormone (LH)

Prolactin

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)

5. Scrotal Ultrasound

Detects varicoceles, testicular tumors or blockages

Assesses blood flow and structural abnormalities

6. Genetic Testing

Karyotype analysis for chromosomal issues

Y-chromosome microdeletion studies

Cystic fibrosis gene mutations (especially if congenital absence of the vas deferens is suspected)

7. Additional Tests

Post-ejaculatory urinalysis: Checks for sperm in the urine (retrograde ejaculation)

Infection screening: Urine and blood tests for STIs

What Can Help: Treatment Options

Many men improve sperm count and quality through targeted therapies and lifestyle adjustments. Your doctor will tailor a plan based on your test results and underlying cause.

Lifestyle Changes

[Doctors Check](https://barrierwatch.bbsxyxy.com/)

Stop smoking and limit alcohol

Maintain a healthy weight (BMI 18.5–24.9)

Wear loose-fitting underwear and avoid hot baths or saunas

Reduce stress with mindfulness, therapy or exercise

Get 7–9 hours of sleep per night

Optimize nutrition with a balanced diet rich in fruits, vegetables, lean protein and whole grains

Nutritional Supplements

Evidence suggests certain antioxidants and nutrients may support sperm health:

Vitamin C and E

Zinc and selenium

Coenzyme Q10

L-carnitine

Folate

Always discuss supplements with your doctor to avoid interactions.

Medical Treatments

Hormonal therapies:

Clomiphene citrate to boost FSH and LH

Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) injections

Testosterone replacement only in select cases (note: external testosterone can lower sperm count)

Antibiotics: To treat documented infections

Medication adjustments: Switching or reducing drugs known to impair fertility

Surgical Interventions

Varicocele repair (ligation or embolization)

Vas deferens reconstruction for obstructions

Testicular sperm extraction (TESE) when no sperm appear in ejaculate

Assisted Reproductive Techniques (ART)

When natural conception remains challenging:

Intrauterine insemination (IUI)

In vitro fertilization (IVF)

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), injecting a single sperm into the egg

These methods bypass many barriers and often lead to successful pregnancies.

Natural Ways to Support Sperm Health

While medical evaluation and treatment are key, everyday choices also play a role:

Stay hydrated—dehydration can affect semen volume.

Eat antioxidant-rich foods: berries, nuts, leafy greens.

Exercise regularly but avoid excessive endurance sports.

Limit exposure to environmental toxins: use gloves when handling chemicals.

Practice safe sex to reduce infection risk.

When to See a Doctor

Speak with a healthcare provider if you experience:

Inability to conceive after 12 months of regular, unprotected intercourse (or 6 months if you're over 35)

Pain, swelling or a palpable lump in the testicles or scrotum

Erectile dysfunction or decreased libido

Signs of hormonal imbalance (fatigue, mood changes, muscle loss)
