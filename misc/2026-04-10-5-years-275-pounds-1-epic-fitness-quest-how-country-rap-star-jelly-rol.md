# 5 Years, 275 Pounds, 1 Epic Fitness Quest: How Country-Rap Star Jelly Roll Transformed His Body

JELLY ROLL FISTS a handful of loose skin beneath his black compression shirt and moves it from his hip over toward his belly, pulling it out of the way. He takes my left hand and wedges it in there, planting it on his iliac crest, the top of the hip bone. I can feel the bone’s definition. He reaches down between my legs and claws the inside of my left thigh with his huge hand.

“I will 1,000 percent not hurt you—especially if you keep that neck stiff when you land so you don’t bang your head,” he says.

His tone is relaxed but also a little intense, like a dad coaching his kid to go off the diving board for the first time.

“You ready?”

Oh, yeah, definitely, I tell him, trying to sound extremely cool with the fact that he is about to body-slam me. To be fair, I volunteered to be body-slammed. I’m here to write about both body and soul, and a body slam seems like a good way to place my trust in both—in the newfound strength and power of his emerging physique, and in the goodness of his heart, which has always been there but which receded for more than a decade behind a tonnage of cocaine, prison time, self-hatred, and fat.

[Star Jelly Roll](https://weight-signal.sunsimiaosd.com/blog/4279402190.html)

When you’re getting body-slammed, putting your hand on the body slammer’s hip bone is important, because in the choreographed mechanics of professional wrestling, you assist the body slammer by pushing off his hip bone as he’s heaving you up over his head, making it easier for him to get you up in the air before dropping you. The hip bone move is a little trick that happens quickly and sneakily—you can also push off from the guy’s quad, but the hip is better because you can really press on it, almost like pushing off the ground for a handspring.

Jelly loves wrestling and MMA, and he’s friends with a lot of those guys. He even joined the massive WWE event SummerSlam 2025 at MetLife Stadium in New Jersey, teaming with Randy “The Viper” Orton to fight Drew McIntyre and the prolific YouTuber Logan Paul. (Paul body-slammed him into an announcer’s table.) He’s delivered body slams before, but now it’s different. More fun, because his body can move better and he just overall feels better. He demonstrates for me a few times the best way to land, with that neck stiff so your head doesn’t pound the mat. He plops down into an athletic sitting position and practices falling back while not letting his head drop. He’s talking the whole time, and not wheezing, and when he’s done, he bounces back up onto his feet.

The body slam itself? I weigh 160—sometimes the heaviest weight I lift all day is this laptop—and for him, it seemed as easy as body-slamming a paper clip. The landing was easy. My neck was a little sore for an hour, that’s it.

When I first met him, in February 2025, he was 380 pounds, down 160 pounds from his heaviest, in 2020. He hadn’t seen or felt the definition of his hip bones in years. Now it’s November. He’s 265—and hopping around the ring looking spry.

Jelly Roll became famous as a country singer, and not just a country singer, but a big, fat country singer with face tattoos, and not just famous but quickly, monumentally famous. He won the Country Music Award for New Artist of the Year in 2023, when he was already 39 years old (and weighed more than 500 pounds); he has been nominated for three Grammys this year, including Best Country Album for Beautifully Broken. Five and a half million people follow him on Instagram and 21 million people listen to him on Spotify, and he has toured the United States, Europe, and Australia—this year.

Now, deep into his “journey,” it’s clear that this journey might end up being the most famous thing he will ever do.

We all set goals, big ones and small ones, and we work toward them, we fail, we try again, we fail again, we give up, we feel bad about ourselves. But sometimes we achieve our goals, and success always, always comes down to how badly we want it. Jelly Roll? He was trying to achieve one of the hardest goals of all: He was trying to lose weight.

Now that he’s done it, the question he’s getting asked a lot is: How?

A lot of spectators and self-appointed commentators—some openly, on social media, and sometimes in a weirdly accusatory way—assume he used GLP-1 medication. While he has nothing against those drugs, he did not. [Editor's note: After MH went to press, Jelly clarified that he did briefly try weight-loss meds very early on his journey, but stopped after two weeks.] We talked for several hours at intervals over the course of this year about how he was doing it, and how he did it. Toward the end of our last conversation, he distilled his method, his focus, into one word:

“Food, food, food, food, food.” But of course, that’s only part of the story.

It’s instructive to know a little about the person he was before he set the goal. Jelly’s father ran a meat company, turning a one-truck beef operation into a wholesale business offering beef, pork, and poultry in Antioch in Nashville. Jelly had been addicted to food for as long as he could remember. He ate constantly, very often at Waffle House, but really, he ate anything. He was also addicted to alcohol, cocaine, and codeine. He also had an early addiction to money, which became an addiction to feeling important.

“Around eighth grade, I discovered that there was lots of ways to make money,” he says. He’s sitting in a lounge chair in the basement of his big new house near Nashville, with rolling lawns and a pasture for his two bulls and two donkeys. “It started with buying candy from the dollar store near my school. Then I’d go sell it at lunch. Then I end up getting a pager and getting some homies. The next thing you know, people see potential in you and they start feeding another direction. Kids start smoking weed around the eighth grade. I happened to be able to find some of it.” Eighth grade was also around the time his parents divorced.

He sold drugs, and he got in deep: The next decade of his life was a mess of drug deals and jail time and gangs. He shakes his head and says, “Totally took a different path. Thought I was something I wasn’t. And I was not that person, man. I am not that man. But if you’d asked me then, I’d have died proving it to you. I’d let you kill me to prove to you I was tough.”

Five-forty. That was his highest weight, where he was about five years ago. More than a quarter-ton. That’s what he estimates, anyway. The scale he used to weigh himself when he started was only calibrated up to 520 pounds, and that needle was pinned to the right. “The needle went past 520, but it didn’t blow it down,” he says. “And I would play with it—I would teeter the foot up, like, how low could I get it? I was mad optimistic. So it could have been 560, it could have been 528.”

His first step was mental. Before anything, he found a therapist he liked. He went after the addiction in ways similar to how he had stopped using cocaine and alcohol. “Even before I got into getting my blood work done, I went and got mental health therapy about my overeating. I started treating my food addiction like what it was: an addiction. Why did I treat cocaine a certain way? I went to meetings for cocaine and found a sponsor and detoxed off of it and shit myself and went through real hard life-changing emotional choices to get off cocaine and codeine,” he says. “I didn’t look at the food addiction different. Once I started treating food like an addiction, it started changing everything for me. When I started really looking at the source of why I was eating. What was I eating for?”

He made perhaps his most important discovery about his goal before he ever changed anything about his diet: His goal was not, in fact, to lose weight, but to get healthy. If he was healthier, he would lose weight. In 2021, to start getting healthy, he first worked with Gary Brecka, the influential podcast host, biologist, and founder of The Ultimate Human, a health and weight-loss “movement” that counts Joe Rogan and Dana White among its followers. Before his first meeting with Brecka, Jelly tried to get below 500 pounds.

“I was so fat that I took that weekend and I fasted and just ate a couple of chicken noodle soups. When I came in under 500, I was like, ‘I’m finally under 500 pounds!’ But I was privately thinking, Dude, fuck, I’m sending a grown man a picture of me on the scale at almost 500 pounds.” Jelly captioned the photo: “I guess we’ve gotta start somewhere.”

Brecka became his advisor in the early weeks, but Jelly knew he wanted to work with a medical team, too, and he found his at Ways2Well, a wellness clinic with locations in Austin and Houston. He told the company’s founder, an entrepreneur and former pharmaceutical rep named Brigham Buhler, that he didn’t want to use Ozempic or any other GLP-1. “He said, ‘When I do this, I don’t want an asterisk next to my name, bubba,’ ” Buhler says. “I want to show people that this is possible.”

A blood panel revealed several factors that, if left undetected and uncorrected, would have made Jelly’s journey a lot longer. (A sidenote is that when Jelly says Ways2Well in his thick and charming Tennessee drawl, it sounds like Ways2Whale.)

First, his insulin levels were “super high,” he says. “You’ll see heavy guys struggling for a long time in the early part because they’re not fast enough to strike that insulin down. And it takes so long to do it.” It was one of the first markers targeted by Danese Rexroad, the nurse practitioner who treated him at Ways2Well.

“Insulin is designed to help glucose enter the cell,” she says. “When you have an excessive amount of insulin, it forces your body to store fat. So switching those receptors and switching what Jelly’s body was signaling with insulin helped him rapidly adjust his insulin resistance. I wasn’t doing a big science experiment. It was just meal timing and eating real foods.” She also started him on metformin, a pharmaceutical used primarily to treat type 2 diabetes that Rexroad hoped would reduce his triglycerides (fat cells in the blood) and improve apoptosis (the process of cells dying in the body).

“My testosterone level—and I’m cool to talk about this openly—was of a preteen boy,” Jelly says. Normal levels for a man Jelly’s age start around 300 ng/dL and go up to 900, but obesity is associated with low T levels, 300 ng/dL and lower. “When I went in there for the test, it was bad. Bad. The world opened up when I seen it on paper. I was like, That’s my testosterone level? I mean, dude, we’re talking a 57.”

He’s been married to his wife, a blonde bombshell named Bunnie Xo, since 2016, and weighing 500 pounds with low testosterone was a form of torture. “You can’t get it up without T,” he says. “I was married to a smoke show, and I was still struggling.”

His cholesterol was high. His A1C, which measures sugar in the blood, was way high. “The first couple of blood panels were like, how are you alive?” he says.

Ways2Well is run on a promise to correct root causes rather than simply medicate symptoms. Ninety percent of the business, Buhler says, is doing blood panels and turning unhealthy results healthy using widely accepted approaches like eating whole foods rich in the nutrients a patient is lacking. They don’t accept insurance, which Buhler says makes the company more accountable to its clients: “We don’t just bill an insurance company every month and coast while the patient is chronically ill. When somebody’s using their cash and they don’t get results, they’re going to fire us fast. And they should.”

Buhler gives an example of the difference between doctors who simply prescribe drugs to mask symptoms and those who correct underlying problems so the symptoms go away:

“If you have high blood pressure, you’re going to be given high blood pressure medication, right?” he says. “Why is your blood pressure high? That’s your body giving clues that something’s wrong. You have to peel back the layers and figure out what’s causing it. In Jelly’s case, why would his body send signals that his insulin needed to be so high? He wasn’t absorbing nutrients because his gut health wasn’t ideal. So we start with the basics, and it sounds simple, but: eating well, moving, sleeping, managing your stress. All of that is curative, but it’s really the foundation of health.”

A team was taking shape. Jelly began hormone therapy (“I’ll be on testosterone replacement therapy probably for the rest of my life”), and his levels were starting to even out. He was becoming aware of what was happening in his blood and in his organs. “That was a big part of my journey: wanting to know what’s happening within me.”

People blame obese people for being obese. The truth, according to Jelly Roll, is that no one loads more blame on an obese person than the obese person.

“It was never-ending sadness,” he says. “And anger. I was a prisoner to my own body. Dude, wiping my ass was a problem. Washing myself properly was a problem. Getting in cars. Every decision I made in life had to be based on my weight. If it could hold me, facilitate me, or fit me—people don’t think about every facet of ‘I still want to be able to do that and I can’t.’ I was so inspired by that kind of stuff.”

It was only part of why he hated the person he had become by his early 20s. “I felt guilty about every decision I made in my teenage years, and that my default was to double down on them because I would have been embarrassed to back out of them,” he says. “The ego of a young man had me wanting to be a thug so bad, had me wanting to be connected with the street so bad. Had me wanting to be something I wasn’t. It took 12, 15 years of being in and out of the system and going through shit and dealing with drugs and drug addicts to realize, man, that was never actually who I was.”

Before all that, when he was a hefty 10-year-old who liked to play football and baseball (all the other boys on the middle school football team were Black, and all the other boys on the baseball team were Hispanic; “It’s how I am who I am. I thank God I grew up that way”), he saw a dead body for the first time. A boy his age drowned in an accident. Jelly went to the funeral and saw the boy and touched his face. One of the new teachers at the middle school was standing outside, and to Jelly it looked like she didn’t want to go in. He encouraged her to go, and he went with her.

“Even that little boy was a lot more cheerful, a lot more joyful, a lot more happy to be around than the obese person I became, who was in and out of the system,” he says. “I don’t know how I turned into that person later.”

About the face tattoos: the cross under his right eye, the teardrops, the music man across his hairline. People chastise him online about them, accusing him of wanting attention. He laughs at this.

“It’s the polar opposite,” he says. “The way shame sometimes shows itself is not always the way we expect it to be. We think shame is somebody in the streets on their knees, with their head down. But shame sometimes is pride. Sometimes shame is just bravado. Behind real bravado, I can normally find shame. I can normally find guilt. I can normally find insecurity. I was the biggest, the loudest, the toughest, the meanest, the growliest, the fattest. There’s normally deep inside of that a really, really small, insecure human. I was that, for sure. Then a by-product of that was I got fat as fuck.”

He may remove the face tattoos. Part of the journey.

His previous false starts at losing weight—and there were many—were false, he believes, because he swore he would do too much at once, and he ended up doing nothing at all. “A lot of dudes get to their bottom dollar, and we’re like, ‘I’m changing! Tomorrow in the morning when I wake up, I’m a different person!’ We attack it all at once. ‘I’m gonna run! I’m gonna lift! I’m gonna eat right. I’m gonna do this and this and this.’ Listen, man, because I’ve done this before: Just pick one of those. And you know which one you need to pick? Food. Start there. Fuck everything else. Just commit yourself to ‘I’m gonna count every calorie and macro that goes in my mouth.’ ”

In 2021, Jelly hired a chef and sports nutritionist named Ian Larios. He’s an MMA fan and ended up working with some MMA champions, which is how Jelly found him.

Larios didn’t want to watch his new client lose, gain, lose again, then gain again, but that’s how things started. At one point, Larios had to tell Jelly he might drop him because it wasn’t working. “Jelly works in a high-pressure environment, touring all the time, and food was his addiction. It brought him comfort. And it’s tough to kick. It wasn’t an overnight thing where he woke up one morning and was fully motivated. It took lots of stumbles, and we still have some,” Larios says.

He worked with Rexroad at Ways2Well to treat the whole Jelly Roll. “We set small goals and we met every few months,” she says. “I told him, ‘We’re going to try to aim for lower insulin, better cholesterol, better sleep. These are the goals that we’re going to hit. It doesn’t mean that in three months you’re going to feel amazing and everything is correct.’ ”

Larios moved into Jelly’s house and cooks for him most days. He’s had a custom mobile kitchen constructed for tours. Using unprocessed ingredients, he developed meals he knew Jelly would like and that would lower inflammation and give his body the nutrients in which it was deficient, like magnesium. Jelly’s favorite Waffle House item is the hash brown bowl: “double hash browns,” American cheese, scrambled eggs, sausage patties, and onions, peppers, and tomatoes. Almost every morning, Larios makes his own rendition: He cooks potatoes in bone broth and air-fries them, so they’re rich in protein and collagen. He makes chicken sausage from scratch and roasts vegetables into a vitamin-rich, sugarless ketchup.

“There is no bad food in our plan, but if you eat too much of any food, it could be bad,” Larios says. “You can overconsume good calories, too. So it was just creating thought patterns for him where it’s like, ‘Let’s create good habits with good food and go from there and move our bodies.’ That was step one.”

Jelly was methodical. He told Larios that his goal was to become healthy and that losing weight would simply be a result. And, as far back as 2022, he told Larios about another goal: He wanted to be on the cover of Men’s Health. “I was like, ‘That’s nuts,’ ” Larios says. In December 2024 Jelly went on Bunnie’s podcast and went public with his goal "to be on the cover of Men's Health in 2026." He’d read Men’s Health when in jail and was inspired by the workouts and transformation stories and wanted his journey, and all his shame and struggles, to be as open and accessible as possible.

Jelly is guest-hosting Jimmy Kimmel Live! for a couple of summer nights. He’s sitting in the greenroom with IV fluids running into a vein in his arm—a quick way to stay hydrated when he’s on the road, which is almost always. A plate of fruit sits untouched by the door.

It’s been a few months since our first meeting, and he looks as if he’s shed about 50 pounds—he reports that he has consistently been losing between 9 and 15 pounds each month. “Dude, I told you! I said, ‘Every time you see me, it’s gonna keep getting dramatic,’ ” he says. His clothing stylist, Krista Roser, is busy steaming a jacket. He asks her to come over and measure the circumference of his stomach. He leans forward so she can wrap the tape measure around him.

“Fifty-three and a half,” she says.

“And do you have what that measurement was at the CMAs?” he asks, referring to the Country Music Awards, which took place just eight months before. Krista types on her laptop and calls out, “You were 69.” His eyebrows go up.

Yesterday morning, before his first Kimmel show, he woke up and did four miles. He likes Coldwater Canyon Park. (“The little, little bitty 0.2-mile lap right there in Beverly Hills next to the fire station. I like it ’cause it’s real pretty.”) He runs the first mile and a half, walks a half-mile, then alternates two-tenths running, two-tenths walking. “When this journey started, I couldn’t get a full mile in in that 30 minutes,” he says. “Now I could put on a pair of tennis shoes, walk out that door, do a mile loop around Hollywood Boulevard, and be back in 12 minutes and 25 seconds. You feel me?”

Krista is still looking at her laptop and reminds him of another measurement from the CMAs: “Your waist was a 65. And you’re close to a 42 now.” “That’s crazy,” Jelly says. “That’s a whole human.”

He works with a physiotherapist daily to help his body learn how to move again. He feels his shoulders, once forced into a constant forward hunch, sliding back. He feels his hips expanding, no longer buckling under the weight.

Larios is here, and he’s got the mobile kitchen. They had lunch at 2, and dinner’s at 8. Jelly might have a piece of fruit before the show, to keep his energy up. But he might not need it. Or even want it.

Back in Nashville in November, the day of the gentle body slam, Jelly is back in his basement, drinking his double-digit bottle of the day of Mountain Valley water. His big plan for the night is that he’s going to shave the beard he’s had for as long as he could grow facial hair.

Jelly is a little nervous—he doesn’t know how prominent his double chin will hang. Bunnie will be there, upstairs in the bathroom, filming for Instagram.

Bunnie, the smoke show for whom he once struggled to get it up. “Now it’s a totally different thing. I’m chasing her around the house, you know what I’m saying?” he says, laughing his deep, hoarse laugh. “I’m like a teenage kid again! I’m like the Pink Panther: I bust out of every corner. And she opens the cabinet and I go, ‘Hi!’ ” It’s got to be fun, this new life.

His head bounces.

“Dramatically different world.”

He still works with Ways2Well, where he has received IV exosomes, a stem cell treatment intended to reduce inflammation and repair tissue. Next, Rexroad will introduce peptides, amino acids purported to assist in reducing inflammation, boosting energy levels, and managing weight loss. Sometime next year, he plans to have his loose skin removed.

Jelly is wearing a roomy black T-shirt and sweats. He looks, well, not obese. He pulls up his right sleeve and makes a muscle: His biceps are discernible. His deltoid is round and firm, a massive boulder that emerged from beneath a glacier.

“These shoulders are mine. Meaning they’re only gonna get bigger. There’s no more fat to lose in them,” he says. “They were gone. I had never seen them.”

He holds the sleeve up longer than you’d think, and I realize he’s doing it for me, but he’s also looking down at his shoulder himself. He used to avoid mirrors, and now he’s pulling up his sleeve to admire his own shoulder. After a few more seconds, he snaps out of it, looks up, and smiles.

“Just a dramatically different world.”
