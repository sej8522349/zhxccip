Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

A pinched nerve occurs when surrounding tissues compress a nerve, disrupting its function and causing sharp pain, tingling, numbness, or muscle weakness. Common causes include herniated discs, bone spurs, repetitive motion, and poor posture.

Doctors diagnose a pinched nerve using physical exams, imaging studies (like MRI or CT scans), and electrodiagnostic tests such as EMG. Recovery time varies from a few days to several months, depending on severity and treatment approach.

Explanation

A pinched nerve occurs when surrounding tissues—such as bones, cartilage, muscles or tendons—apply too much pressure to a nerve. This pressure disrupts nerve function, causing pain, tingling, numbness or weakness. Understanding common symptoms, how doctors diagnose a pinched nerve and typical recovery times can help you manage your discomfort and know when to seek medical care.

What Causes a Pinched Nerve?

A variety of factors can lead to nerve compression:

Herniated or bulging discs in the spine

Bone spurs from arthritis

Repetitive motions (e.g., typing, heavy lifting)

Poor posture or awkward sleeping positions

Injury or trauma to a joint or muscle

Obesity, increasing pressure on nerves

Certain jobs, hobbies or sports that involve repetitive motions can make you more prone to developing a pinched nerve.

Common Symptoms Doctors See

Pain Patterns

Sharp, burning or aching pain radiating along the nerve path

Pain that worsens with specific movements (turning the head, bending forward)

Pain that improves when you rest or change positions

Sensory Changes

Numbness or decreased sensation in the area served by the nerve

Tingling or "pins and needles" sensations (paresthesia)

Electric shock–like sensations

Muscle Signs

Weakness in the muscles controlled by the affected nerve

Trouble gripping objects, lifting your foot (foot drop) or turning your head

Muscle spasms or cramping

How Doctors Diagnose a Pinched Nerve

Onset, duration and pattern of symptoms

Any recent injuries, repetitive activities or changes in work habits

Neurological exam: testing strength, reflexes and sensation

Special maneuvers (Spurling's test for cervical nerves, straight-leg raise for sciatica)

X-rays to check for bone spurs or alignment issues

MRI to visualize discs, nerves and soft tissues

CT scan for more detailed bone images

How Long Does a Pinched Nerve Last?

Recovery time varies by cause, severity and treatment:

Mild cases (due to temporary posture or minor strain)• Improvement in days to weeks with rest and self-care

Moderate cases (disc bulge or mild repetitive stress injury)• 4–12 weeks for significant symptom relief with conservative treatment

Severe cases (large herniated disc, significant nerve compression)• May require 3–6 months or longer, especially if surgery is needed

Most people start to feel better within a few weeks of beginning treatment. Full recovery can take longer, particularly if muscles need to regain strength.

Conservative Treatment Options

Doctors often recommend non­surgical approaches first:

Rest and Activity Modification• Avoid positions or tasks that worsen symptoms• Short breaks and gentle movement rather than prolonged inactivity

Ice and Heat Therapy• Ice packs (first 48 hours) to reduce inflammation• Heat pads to relax tight muscles and improve blood flow

Over-the-Counter Pain Relievers• NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) for pain and swelling• Acetaminophen for pain relief if NSAIDs are contraindicated

Physical Therapy• Stretching and strengthening exercises• Posture training and ergonomic adjustments

Cervical or Lumbar Traction• Gentle stretching of the spine to relieve pressure on nerves

Bracing or Splinting (for carpal tunnel or elbow compression)• Keeps wrist/elbow in a neutral position to reduce nerve stress

When to Consider Advanced Treatments

If conservative measures fail after 6–12 weeks or symptoms worsen:

Corticosteroid Injections• Reduce inflammation around the nerve root• Provide temporary relief while you continue physical therapy

Surgery• Discectomy or laminectomy to remove herniated disc material or bone spurs• Carpal tunnel release for severe wrist compression• Generally considered when there is significant weakness, loss of bladder/bowel control, or unrelenting pain

Self-Care and Prevention Tips

Maintain good posture when sitting, standing and driving

Take frequent breaks during repetitive tasks

Use ergonomic workstations: keyboard at elbow height, monitor at eye level

Practice neck and back stretches daily

Keep a healthy weight to reduce spine and joint stress

Wake up with a supportive pillow and mattress

When to Seek Immediate Medical Help

Although most pinched nerves improve with time and conservative care, certain signs warrant urgent evaluation:

Sudden, severe weakness in an arm or leg

Loss of bladder or bowel control

Severe, unrelenting pain that prevents any movement

Signs of infection (fever, redness, warmth over the spine)
