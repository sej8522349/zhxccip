What is Ferritin?

Ferritin is your body's main storage protein for iron. It's found inside cells throughout your body, especially in the liver, spleen, bone marrow, and skeletal muscle. Each ferritin molecule can hold thousands of iron atoms in a safe, non-toxic form.

Iron's cellular vault

When you absorb iron from food, your body doesn't let it float freely in the bloodstream - that would cause damage. Instead, cells package it inside ferritin for safekeeping. A small amount of ferritin circulates in your blood, and that level reflects how much iron is stored in your tissues.

A window into your iron reserves

Measuring ferritin gives doctors a reliable snapshot of your total body iron stores. Low ferritin signals depleted reserves, often before anemia develops. High ferritin can indicate iron overload, inflammation, or other metabolic conditions.

More than just an iron marker

Because ferritin also rises during inflammation and infection, it serves as an acute-phase reactant. This dual role makes it useful beyond iron assessment, helping clinicians evaluate immune activation and systemic stress.

What do my Ferritin results mean?

Low ferritin usually reflects depleted iron stores

Low values usually reflect depleted iron stores, the body's reserve supply of iron held primarily in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. This depletion occurs before anemia develops and signals that the body is drawing down its reserves to maintain red blood cell production and other iron-dependent processes. Common causes include inadequate dietary intake, poor absorption from the gut, chronic blood loss, or increased demand during pregnancy and rapid growth. Women of reproductive age are particularly vulnerable due to menstrual blood loss. Low ferritin impairs oxygen delivery, energy production in mitochondria, and immune function, often causing fatigue, weakness, and reduced exercise tolerance.

Being in range suggests adequate iron reserves

Being in range suggests adequate iron reserves to support red blood cell formation, oxygen transport, and cellular energy metabolism. Optimal ferritin levels tend to sit in the mid to upper portion of the reference range, supporting stable hemoglobin production and metabolic efficiency without excess storage.

High ferritin usually reflects inflammation or iron overload

High values usually reflect either acute or chronic inflammation, where ferritin rises as an acute phase reactant, or true iron overload from genetic conditions like hemochromatosis, repeated transfusions, or excessive supplementation. Elevated ferritin can also accompany liver disease, metabolic syndrome, and certain malignancies.

Context matters for interpretation

Ferritin interpretation requires clinical context. Inflammation from infection, autoimmune disease, or tissue injury can elevate ferritin even when iron stores are low, masking deficiency.
