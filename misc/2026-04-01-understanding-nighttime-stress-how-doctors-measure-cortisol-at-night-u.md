Nighttime stress can disrupt sleep quality, metabolism, and overall health. A key marker of stress is cortisol—a hormone your body releases in response to physical or emotional tension. In this article, we explain how doctors track cortisol overnight, why sleep disorders (including sleep apnea) influence cortisol levels, and what you can do to keep stress in check without panicking.

What Is Cortisol and Why Does It Matter at Night?

Cortisol is often called the "stress hormone," but it has many normal, healthy roles:

Regulates blood sugar and energy

Supports immune responses

Helps control inflammation

Aids memory formation

Cortisol follows a natural 24-hour cycle called the circadian rhythm. Under typical conditions:

Levels peak in the early morning (around waking time)

Gradually drop throughout the day

Reach their lowest point between midnight and 4 AM

When this cycle is disrupted—by chronic stress, shift work, or sleep disorders—cortisol can spike at night, leaving you restless and affecting:

Blood pressure

Blood sugar control

Mood and memory

Weight management

How Doctors Measure Nighttime Cortisol

Clinicians have several tools to see how your cortisol behaves when you're supposed to be sleeping.

1. Late-Night Salivary Cortisol

Why it's used: Noninvasive, easy to collect at home

How it works: You spit into a tube around 11 PM or midnight

What it tells us: Elevated levels at night often point to HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) axis overactivity

2. Overnight Blood Sampling

Why it's used: Gold standard for precision

How it works: A small catheter stays in your arm; samples are taken every hour or at specific time points

What it tells us: Hourly profiles show exact times of cortisol peaks and valleys

3. 24-Hour Urinary Free Cortisol

Why it's used: Reflects total cortisol production over a full day-night cycle

How it works: You collect all urine for 24 hours, then lab tests quantify cortisol breakdown products

What it tells us: High total cortisol can indicate Cushing's syndrome or sustained stress

4. Dexamethasone Suppression Test (DST)

Why it's used: Helps diagnose Cushing's syndrome

How it works: You take a low dose of dexamethasone (a synthetic steroid) in the evening; cortisol is measured the next morning

What it tells us: In healthy individuals, cortisol should suppress; if it doesn't, further testing is needed

Sleep Apnea and Cortisol Spikes

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep disorder where breathing repeatedly stops and starts. This interruption triggers a stress response:

Oxygen drops: Brief oxygen deprivation activates the HPA axis

Arousals: Even micro-wakes raise cortisol to restart breathing

Fragmented sleep: Prevents normal nighttime cortisol decline

Studies show that people with untreated sleep apnea often have:

Higher late-night and early-morning cortisol levels

Greater daytime fatigue and mood disturbances

Increased risk of hypertension, diabetes, and heart disease

Managing sleep apnea can help normalize cortisol rhythms:

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)

Oral appliances or surgery

Weight loss and positional therapy

Why Nighttime Cortisol Spikes Matter

Consistently high cortisol at night can contribute to or worsen:

Insomnia: Difficulty falling or staying asleep

Metabolic issues: Elevated blood sugar, insulin resistance

Weight gain: Especially around the abdomen

Cardiovascular risks: High blood pressure, artery damage

Mood disturbances: Anxiety, depression, irritability

Balanced cortisol is as important as getting enough hours of sleep. When stress hormones remain elevated after bedtime, your body never fully shifts into restorative, deep sleep stages.

Go to bed and wake up at the same times daily

Wind down 30 minutes before bed with calming activities (reading, gentle stretching)

Limit Late Stimulants

Avoid caffeine after mid-afternoon

Minimize alcohol close to bedtime (it can fragment sleep)

Create a Sleep-Friendly Environment

Keep your bedroom cool (60–67°F) and dark

Use white noise or earplugs if needed to mask disruptions

Practice Relaxation Techniques

Deep-breathing exercises or meditation

Progressive muscle relaxation to release tension

Assess for Sleep Disorders

Loud snoring, daytime sleepiness, or gasping at night could signal sleep apnea

When to Talk to a Doctor

If you struggle with persistent insomnia, loud snoring, daytime fatigue, or weight changes despite lifestyle changes, speak with a healthcare professional. They may recommend:

A formal sleep study (polysomnography)

Salivary or blood cortisol testing
