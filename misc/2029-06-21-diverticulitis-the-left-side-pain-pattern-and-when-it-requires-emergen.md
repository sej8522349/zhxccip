Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Diverticulitis is a digestive condition marked by persistent lower left abdominal pain that often intensifies over several hours. Common symptoms include fever, chills, nausea, and changes in bowel habits such as constipation or diarrhea. Seek emergency care immediately for sudden severe pain, high fever, inability to pass gas or stool, persistent vomiting, dehydration, or signs of shock (rapid heartbeat, dizziness, confusion).

Explanation

Diverticulitis occurs when small pouches (diverticula) that form in the lining of the digestive tract become inflamed or infected. While these pouches most often develop in the lower part of the large intestine (colon), they can cause pain anywhere along the colon. A classic sign is persistent discomfort in the lower left side of the abdomen. Understanding diverticulitis symptoms—and knowing when they signal an emergency—can help you get timely care and avoid complications.

What Is Diverticulitis?

Diverticula are small bulges or pockets that can form in the colon wall, especially as we age.

When these pockets become irritated, infected, or tear, the condition is called diverticulitis.

It is different from diverticulosis, which simply means the presence of diverticula without inflammation.

Common Diverticulitis Symptoms

Identifying diverticulitis symptoms early helps guide treatment and prevent serious complications. Key warning signs include:

Lower left abdominal painMost people describe a dull, constant ache or tenderness in the lower left quadrant. It can start mild and worsen over hours or days.

Fever and chillsA low-grade fever often accompanies inflammation. Higher fevers may suggest a more serious infection.

Changes in bowel habitsYou may experience constipation, diarrhea, or a feeling that you haven't completely emptied your bowels.

When to Seek Emergency Care

Most mild cases improve with rest, diet changes, and antibiotics under a doctor's guidance. However, seek emergency care if you experience:

Severe or sudden abdominal pain, especially if it's worsening.

High fever (above 101°F or 38.3°C) and chills.

Inability to pass stool, gas, or have a bowel movement.

Persistent vomiting or signs of dehydration (dry mouth, dizziness).

Signs of shock: rapid heartbeat, low blood pressure, fainting.

Rapid swelling of the abdomen (suggesting a blockage or abscess).

Diagnosis and Tests

To confirm diverticulitis and rule out other conditions (like appendicitis or inflammatory bowel disease), your doctor may recommend:

Medical history and physical examDiscuss your symptoms and press gently on the abdomen to localize pain.

Blood testsElevated white blood cells can indicate infection.

Stool testsCheck for blood or signs of infection.

CT scan (Computed Tomography)The most accurate imaging test for identifying inflamed or infected diverticula, abscesses, and complications.

Treatment and Management

Treatment depends on the severity of your symptoms:

Mild diverticulitis (outpatient care)

Clear liquid diet for a few days, gradually advancing to low-fiber foods.

Oral antibiotics to treat infection.

Pain relief with acetaminophen (avoid NSAIDs like ibuprofen, which can irritate the gut).

Plenty of fluids and rest.

Moderate to severe diverticulitis (hospital care)

Intravenous (IV) antibiotics and fluids.

Bowel rest (nothing by mouth) until inflammation decreases.

CT-guided drainage of any abscesses, if present.

Surgery if complications arise (e.g., perforation, uncontrolled bleeding, or non-healing abscess).

After recovery, your doctor may recommend a high-fiber diet and lifestyle changes to reduce the chance of recurrence.
