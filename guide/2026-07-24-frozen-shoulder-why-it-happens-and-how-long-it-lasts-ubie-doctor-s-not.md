Frozen shoulder, also known as adhesive capsulitis, occurs when the connective tissue surrounding the shoulder joint thickens and tightens, restricting movement. The condition progresses through three distinct stages — freezing, frozen, and thawing — and can last 1 to 3 years if left untreated.

Treatment options include physical therapy, pain relievers, joint distension injections, and, in rare cases, surgery. Key risk factors include age (typically 40–60), diabetes, prolonged shoulder immobilization, and thyroid disorders, all of which can influence recovery time.

Because frozen shoulder shares symptoms with rotator cuff injuries, arthritis, and other conditions, identifying the cause early is critical to choosing the right treatment and avoiding long-term stiffness. Explanation

Frozen shoulder, also known as adhesive capsulitis, is a condition characterized by stiffness, pain, and reduced range of motion in the shoulder joint. It can make everyday activities—like reaching for a shelf or fastening a seatbelt—difficult. Understanding why it happens and how long it lasts can help you manage symptoms and seek appropriate care without undue worry.

What Is Frozen Shoulder?

Frozen shoulder occurs when the connective tissue (capsule) surrounding the shoulder joint becomes thickened and tight. This limits movement and often leads to pain. The condition typically develops in three overlapping stages:

Freezing Stage

Gradual onset of pain, which worsens with movement.

Shoulder range of motion begins to decrease.

Lasts about 6 to 9 months.

Frozen Stage

Pain may lessen, but stiffness remains or worsens.

Daily activities become more challenging.

Lasts about 4 to 12 months.

Thawing Stage

Shoulder movement gradually improves.

Pain continues to fade.

Lasts about 6 months to 2 years.

Overall, frozen shoulder can last anywhere from 1 to 3 years if untreated. With proper care, many people recover sooner.

How It Develops

The shoulder joint is a ball-and-socket joint with a capsule of connective tissue that keeps the bones in place.

When this capsule becomes inflamed, it forms thick scar tissue and contracts, "freezing" the joint.

Over time, the capsule may loosen, allowing movement to return during the thawing stage.

Signs and Symptoms

Symptoms of frozen shoulder tend to develop slowly and worsen over time:

Dull or achy pain deep in the shoulder or upper arm, often worse at night

Stiffness making it hard to reach overhead, behind the back, or out to the side

Limited active (you move) and passive (someone else moves you) range of motion

Pain when applying pressure to the shoulder

Diagnosing Frozen Shoulder

A healthcare professional will typically:

Review your medical history and symptoms.

Perform a physical exam, checking the range of motion actively and passively.

Order imaging tests (X-ray, ultrasound, or MRI) to rule out other conditions such as arthritis or a rotator cuff tear.

Self-Care Strategies

In addition to medical treatment, you can support recovery with home care:

Maintain gentle daily shoulder activity to prevent further stiffness.

Practice prescribed stretches consistently—set reminders if needed.

Use ergonomic adjustments at work (e.g., proper chair height, arm support).

Apply heat before exercises and cold afterward to manage discomfort.

Prioritize good posture: shoulders back, chest open, head aligned over spine.

When to Seek Further Help

Persistent, worsening pain despite home care and therapy.

Signs of infection (fever, redness, warmth) around the shoulder.

Sudden severe pain following injury.

Any new symptoms that feel serious or life threatening.

If you experience any concerning signs, speak with a healthcare provider promptly.

Key Takeaways

Frozen shoulder involves painful stiffness of the shoulder capsule, progressing through freezing, frozen, and thawing stages.

It most often affects people aged 40–60, especially women, and those with diabetes or recent shoulder immobilization.

Recovery can take 1–3 years but often improves sooner with physical therapy, medication, and consistent self-care.
