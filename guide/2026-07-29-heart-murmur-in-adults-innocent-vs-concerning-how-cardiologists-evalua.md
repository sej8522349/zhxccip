Heart Murmur in Adults: Innocent vs. Concerning — How Cardiologists Evaluate Yours

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Heart murmurs in adults can be harmless (innocent) or signal underlying heart disease, such as valve disorders or structural abnormalities. Cardiologists evaluate murmurs by analyzing timing, loudness, location, associated symptoms, and physical exam findings. An accurate diagnosis typically involves a detailed medical history, physical maneuvers, imaging studies like echocardiograms, and targeted lab tests to determine whether reassurance, monitoring, or treatment is required.

Key warning signs include fatigue, shortness of breath, chest discomfort, swelling, dizziness, or palpitations — symptoms that may indicate a more serious cause behind the murmur.

Because heart murmurs range from completely benign to indicators of significant heart conditions, identifying your specific symptoms early is critical to getting the right care at the right time. Explanation

A heart murmur in adults is an extra or unusual sound heard during a heartbeat. Many murmurs are harmless ("innocent") and require no treatment, while others indicate underlying heart conditions that need medical attention. Knowing the difference can ease concerns and guide you toward the right evaluation.

What Is a Heart Murmur?

A heart murmur is produced by turbulent blood flow in or near the heart. During a routine exam, your doctor listens with a stethoscope and notes:

Timing (systolic vs. diastolic)

Loudness (graded I–VI)

Pitch (high vs. low)

Location on the chest

Radiation (where the sound travels)

Innocent (Functional) Murmurs

Innocent murmurs occur when blood flows normally through a healthy heart. They are common and often discovered by chance. Features include:

Soft (grade I–II)

Systolic timing

No other abnormal heart sounds

No symptoms (you feel fine)

Concerning (Pathologic) Murmurs

Pathologic murmurs suggest structural changes or disease. They may be loud, accompanied by other sounds (e.g., clicks), or linked to symptoms. Typical signs:

Grade III–VI intensity

Diastolic or continuous timing

Thrill (vibration) felt on chest wall

Common Causes of Innocent Murmurs in Adults

Mild anemia (low red blood cell count)

Increased blood flow (fever, hyperthyroidism, pregnancy)

High cardiac output states (athletic conditioning)

Normal variation with aging

These murmurs do not damage the heart, and most adults with innocent murmurs live normal lives without restrictions.

How Cardiologists Evaluate a Heart Murmur in Adults

Your cardiologist begins by asking about:

Family history of heart disease

Personal history of rheumatic fever or infections

Current and past symptoms (e.g., chest pain, palpitations)

Medications and lifestyle factors (smoking, diet, activity)

Auscultation with a stethoscope to characterize the murmur

Management and Treatment

Innocent Murmurs

Reassurance: No treatment needed—your heart is healthy.

Periodic checkups: Monitor during routine visits.

Lifestyle guidance: Maintain heart‐healthy habits (exercise, balanced diet, avoid smoking).

Concerning Murmurs

Treatment depends on the underlying cause and severity:

Blood pressure control (ACE inhibitors, beta blockers)

Diuretics to reduce fluid overload

Antibiotics for endocarditis prevention or treatment

Valve repair or replacement (surgical or transcatheter)

Closure devices for septal defects

Ablation for arrhythmias

Regular imaging and follow-up exams

Adjustment of therapy based on symptom changes

Living with a Heart Murmur

Follow your cardiologist's recommendations for lifestyle and medications.

Stay active within your prescribed limits—regular exercise benefits most heart conditions.

Attend all follow‐up appointments and imaging studies.

Alert any healthcare provider (dentist, surgeon) about your murmur or valve condition—some procedures may require antibiotic prophylaxis.

Key Takeaways

A heart murmur in adults can be innocent or a sign of heart disease.

Innocent murmurs are common and harmless; no treatment is needed.

Concerning murmurs often accompany symptoms or structural issues and require detailed evaluation.

Cardiologists use history, exam, imaging, and special tests to determine the cause and guide treatment.

Always report new symptoms promptly and consult a doctor for any potentially serious signs.

If you have concerns about a heart murmur or experience symptoms such as chest pain, fainting, or swelling, please speak to a doctor right away. Early evaluation and treatment can make all the difference.
