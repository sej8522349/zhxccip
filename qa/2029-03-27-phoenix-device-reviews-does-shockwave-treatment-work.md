Effectiveness

In measuring the effectiveness of the Phoenix, we took a close look at the available scientific research investigating the efficacy of shockwave therapy as a treatment. Then, we compared the technical specifications of the Phoenix to those seen in shockwave machines used by clinicians.

Numerous studies support in-office Li-ESWT as a way to improve some varieties of ED, specifically vasculogenic ED. One recent review of studies showed a roughly 30% increase in both International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) scores and Erectile Hardness Scores (EHS) after only three months of shockwave therapy.3

In another study comparing radial and focused shockwave treatments, a 15Hz radial treatment appeared to be moderately effective, with 75% of participants seeing improvements.4 Several other studies looked at lower-frequency shockwave treatments for ED, ranging from 2Hz-6Hz, and saw similar efficacy.

The Phoenix produces a radial shockwave with a frequency range of 15Hz-19Hz. That may seem a little high, given that many studies work with slightly lower frequencies and that many clinical shockwave devices operate up to 15Hz. That’s likely because lower frequencies achieve deeper penetration, which can be helpful to reach an injured hip through a dense layer of body fat, but is unnecessary to affect the tissues and circulation in the average penis.5 That’s why ED shockwave studies see similar efficacy at both 15Hz and 2Hz — 15Hz is low enough.

However, there's also a question regarding at-home devices’ energy output, a difference in which could necessitate a change in frequency to achieve clinically relevant results at home. As Dr. Kelepurovski puts it, "While some of these frequencies may overlap with what is used in clinical settings, the energy flux density, which is another important therapeutic consideration, is not disclosed and will likely differ from clinical adjuncts."

Overall, this indicates the Phoenix may be of value to users who follow the protocol, but independent home use studies would help validate its potential.

Safety

In most cases, shockwave therapy is mostly considered safe when properly administered, especially radial shockwave therapy, which spreads the wave over a wider area than focused shockwave therapy. The Phoenix is a radial shockwave device.

Studies indicate that this therapy produces no side effects, though it’s important to note that these studies used clinical shockwave devices, not the Phoenix itself.6 The Phoenix is an electromechanical shockwave device, so its operation creates a significant amount of noise. At approximately 88dB, it’s a little louder than the average blender or battery-operated drill.

Chronic exposure to such loud volumes can damage your hearing.7 The reported hearing discomfort or damage from high-decibel shockwave therapy in non-penile areas, like the upper back or neck, is purely anecdotal. One study looked at the phenomenon acutely, but it did so for patients with plantar fasciitis, so the therapy was aimed at the foot.8 That may have reduced the intensity of the volume that reached participants’ ears.

Ultimately, as long as you follow your protocol closely and wear adequate ear protection, these devices should be safe. (Many ear plugs on the market advertise up to 30dB of protection, which should suffice in this case.)

Because this device requires self-administration, there is a chance for improper technique or misuse in a way that could affect safety or results. Clear instructions and user education matter even more in these instances.

How shockwaves can treat ED

It’s important to clarify that shockwave therapy is not the same as electroshock or electrical stimulation therapy. It does not electrically stimulate you in any way. Rather, it provides a pulse of sound waves tuned to a certain frequency and delivered with enough intensity to essentially massage tissues that you couldn’t otherwise reach.9 And the frequencies used in shockwave therapy have been studied in various clinical settings, including musculoskeletal and urological applications.10

Extracorporeal shockwave therapy has been a popular tool in pain management for some time, with certain forms used to treat injuries closer to the surface of the body and more focused applications capable of deeper penetration to do things like break up kidney stones. As you might conclude, that kind of deep-penetrating shockwave therapy isn’t ideal for penile applications.

Instead, a low-intensity form of the therapy, Li-ESWT, is applied along the outside of the penis to stimulate tissue repair and blood flow in the complex of muscles and blood vessels responsible for achieving and maintaining an erection.11

How does the Phoenix create shockwaves?

There are several ways the shockwave devices create their therapeutic output. Some use electromagnetism or piezoelectricity, which are accurate but expensive approaches. The Phoenix uses an electromechanical system to create shockwaves, a method that is also utilized in certain clinical devices, though frequently with distinct treatment protocols and energy settings.

Essentially, a small but powerful motor repeatedly slams a kind of bullet housed in the cylinder of the device against a metal plate. The components are tuned so that the resulting vibration from this impact travels down the cylinder and creates a shockwave emitting from the device’s tip.

Mechanically, this is not unlike how certain power tools work, using the kind of rotating mechanism found in some power drills and nail guns. The volume output is also similar, with both the motor and the impact of components combining to produce an output of around 88dB.

Is the Phoenix safe?

Ultimately, the Phoenix is safe when used correctly. Most studies looking into the safety and efficacy of Li-ESWT conclude that it’s safe for everything from plantar fasciitis to erectile dysfunction.6 However, these studies are performed by trained clinicians. No study currently exists evaluating the safety of at-home, self-administered shockwave therapy.

Fortunately, the protocol is simple enough that most men should be able to follow it without difficulty. You should be fine if you stick to that protocol and use the device carefully. Some men find the treatment sessions slightly uncomfortable, and for that, the company recommends either a lidocaine or benzocaine-based numbing product designed for penile use, a small amount of lubricant, or both.13 The company sells its own versions of each, but we also have comprehensive guides to numbing sprays and wipes that are as effective and a little less pricey than what Phoenix sells.

The intense volume output you’ll experience with the Phoenix is also of some concern, as it’s enough to cause some hearing damage over time. Abuse or deviation could certainly pose higher risks, but users who carefully follow the protocol are unlikely to encounter issues. That said, we recommend using some form of ear protection during your sessions to be extra safe.

It’s also possible that certain medical conditions, such as Peyronie's disease, could make using the Phoenix more hazardous. Li-ESWT has been investigated in preliminary studies for Peyronie's disease; however, safety profiles and ideal protocols are still unknown, particularly for self-administered use.12

There is also a risk of over-treatment for men too eager for their own good to see improvements. But the Phoenix has a 36-hour lockout that initiates after each treatment to prevent this from becoming a reality. And the LCD on the device displays the number of hours remaining until you can perform your next treatment so you know when it’s time to go again. (You don’t have to leave the device plugged in for this feature to work; the lockout timer has its own dedicated battery.)

When those lights go out, you should be at the bottom of your shaft by your pelvis.

Begin moving the tip back up the shaft along the same line, this time following the speed at which the blue lights come on.

When you get back to the top of the shaft, reposition the device at the next treatment point and repeat steps 5-9.

Once you’ve treated all five points (the company calls them “lines of travel”), the 36-hour lockout will engage.

If you picture a clock face with a hole in the middle and imagine inserting your penis into that hole, you’ll see the points at which the lines of travel exist. These treatment sessions last about 17 minutes, and you’ll perform them twice weekly for 30 days, then take 30 days off, and then repeat that pattern until 120 days have passed. After that, you can perform a “maintenance cycle” of 30 days once or twice per year.

Phoenix shockwave therapy FAQ

Does shockwave therapy work for ED?

Clinical trials have shown statistically significant improvement in erectile function with low-intensity extracorporeal shockwave therapy (Li-ESWT).3 Clinically significant improvements were seen in men with mild to moderate vasculogenic ED. Clinical outcomes are heterogeneous, and it is possible effectiveness is reduced in men with more severe or non-vascular ED.

Can shockwave therapy cure ED?

Shockwave therapy won't cure your ED. But some men report longer-lasting benefits after completing an initial protocol, requiring only occasional maintenance sessions. And the long-term results vary by individual.

Is shockwave therapy the same as electroshock therapy?

No. Shockwave therapy produces focused acoustic waves that stimulate tissue and blood flow. ECT with electroshock therapy is a form of electrical stimulation of the brain as part of psychiatric care.13 Electrically stimulating the brain is entirely different.

Is the Phoenix as good as clinical shockwave treatment?

While the Phoenix has underlying technologies similar to a few clinical shockwave devices, there are critical differences, including delivery of energy, operator expertise, and the regulatory framework. It likely has similar benefits, but current research on at-home devices is limited in relation to clinical, professionally administered Li-ESWT.

Is shockwave therapy dangerous?

Shockwave therapy is usually well tolerated and can be safely used when applied appropriately.6 You may feel mild pain, and some users choose to use numbing agents or lubricants. The device is also slightly louder than you may expect, and if you are using this device repeatedly, you may prefer to wear ear protection.
