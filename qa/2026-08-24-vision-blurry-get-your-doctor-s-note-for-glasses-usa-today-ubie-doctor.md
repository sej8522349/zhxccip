Common Causes of Blurry Vision

1. Refractive Errors (Most Common Cause)

Refractive errors happen when light does not focus correctly on the retina. These are usually corrected with prescription glasses or contact lenses.

The main types include:

Nearsightedness (myopia): Clear up close, blurry far away

Farsightedness (hyperopia): Clear far away, blurry up close

Astigmatism: Blurry or distorted vision at any distance

Presbyopia: Age-related difficulty focusing up close (usually after 40)

In the United States, refractive errors are the most common reason people search for glasses USA providers.

2. Digital Eye Strain

Spending hours on phones, tablets, or computers can lead to:

Blurry vision

Dry eyes

Headaches

Neck and shoulder tension

This is sometimes called "computer vision syndrome."

The American Optometric Association recommends the 20-20-20 rule:

Every 20 minutes

Look 20 feet away

For 20 seconds

This simple habit can significantly reduce strain.

4. Eye Conditions That Require Medical Care

Some causes of blurry vision are more serious and should not be ignored:

Cataracts – Clouding of the eye's natural lens

Glaucoma – Damage to the optic nerve (often without early symptoms)

Macular degeneration – Loss of central vision

Diabetic retinopathy – Vision damage from diabetes

Retinal detachment – Sudden flashes, floaters, or curtain-like shadow

If blurry vision is sudden, painful, or accompanied by weakness, slurred speech, or severe headache, seek emergency care immediately. These can signal stroke or other urgent conditions.

When Should You See a Doctor?

Speak to a healthcare professional if you experience:

Sudden vision loss

Double vision

Eye pain

New floaters or flashes

Blurry vision that doesn't improve

Vision changes with diabetes or high blood pressure

Even if symptoms seem mild, routine comprehensive eye exams are recommended:

Every 1–2 years for most adults

Annually if you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of eye disease

Blurry vision is often harmless—but sometimes it is not. A doctor can help determine the difference.
